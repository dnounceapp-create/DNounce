Hiring a freelancer should be simple: find someone talented, agree on terms, get the work done. But the stories of deposits lost, deadlines missed, and deliverables never delivered are everywhere.

The thing is, most of these situations had warning signs that were visible before any money changed hands. Here are five that matter most.

## 1. They Can't Show You Work They Actually Did

A portfolio is table stakes. But portfolios can be faked, borrowed, or outdated. Before you hire, ask to see:

- Work specifically similar to what you need
- The process behind a piece, not just the final result
- A client reference for their most recent relevant project

If they can show you beautiful work but can't tell you anything about how it was made or who it was for, that's a flag.

## 2. Their Communication Is Inconsistent Before You Hire Them

How someone communicates before they have your money is the best preview of how they'll communicate after.

Watch for:
- Takes days to respond to simple questions
- Gives vague answers when you ask for specifics
- Overpromises without asking enough questions about your actual needs

A freelancer who's hard to reach during the sales process will be harder to reach when something goes wrong mid-project.

## 3. The Price Is Dramatically Lower Than Everyone Else

Competitive pricing is real. Suspiciously low pricing is a different thing.

When a freelancer quotes significantly below market rate, one of a few things is usually happening:
- They're inexperienced and undervaluing themselves (which comes with its own risks)
- They're planning to upsell you heavily on "extras" once the project starts
- They're taking on more clients than they can handle

Get three quotes. If one is 40% below the others, ask why.

## 4. They Push You to Decide Before You're Ready

Legitimate freelancers with full calendars don't need to pressure you. "I have another client interested in this slot" is sometimes true and sometimes a tactic.

A good test: tell them you need 48 hours to decide. If they respond with urgency or the "opportunity" disappears, you didn't lose anything worth having.

## 5. There's No Contract — Or They Resist One

This is the clearest signal of all. A freelancer who balks at a written agreement is a freelancer who wants flexibility to reinterpret what was agreed to.

Your contract doesn't have to be complicated. It should cover:
- Exactly what is being delivered
- When it's being delivered
- What payment is tied to what milestone
- What revision rounds are included
- What happens if either party needs to cancel

If they say "we don't need all that, I just do good work" — that's the flag.

## What to Do When Things Go Wrong Anyway

Even with all the right checks, disputes happen. When they do, having a documented record of what was agreed to and what was delivered is everything.

Platforms like [DNounce](https://www.dnounce.com) exist specifically for this: file a record of your experience, give the freelancer a chance to respond publicly, and let the community weigh in. Both sides get heard. The truth tends to win.
